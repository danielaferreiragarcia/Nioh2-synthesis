Do copper crystal shapes change how organics oxidize to VFAs?

What has been published on how the shape, exposed facets and phase of Cu, Cu2O and CuO change activity and carboxylate selectivity when organics are oxidized at a copper anode, and how to run the comparison yourself. A companion to the Ni(OH)2 morphology review.

Compiled October 2026153 records screened, 40 citedFrom abstracts and excerpts

Short answer

No direct study yet

  • No paper compares Cu shapes or facets while measuring formate, acetate or other carboxylate products. Three separate searches aimed at finding one came back empty.
  • The direct Cu2O shape comparisons are glucose sensors that report current, not products. Even there, labs disagree on the winner: cubes in one study, octahedra in another, rhombic dodecahedra in a third.
  • Under anodic bias the as-made facets do not survive. Cu2O surfaces turn into CuO or Cu(OH)2, and phase, defects, pH, potential and the substrate set the products more clearly than the outline shape.
  • On copper anodes the VFA-type products are mostly formate, from C–C cleavage of polyols and sugars, and acetate, from ethanol. Nothing builds C–C bonds, so propionate and butyrate would need C3 and C4 feeds.

The closest comparisons disagree

Shape-controlled Cu2O is easy to make, so cubes, octahedra and rhombic dodecahedra have been compared many times. For organic oxidation every one of those comparisons is a glucose sensor, and the winning facet changes from lab to lab.

Why shape matters less for copper

Copper oxidizes organics in two separate potential windows, and in both the surface you made is not the surface that reacts. Metallic Cu dehydrogenates aldehydes near 0 V vs RHE and releases H2 at the anode, until Cu itself oxidizes near 0.5 V. Polyols, sugars and alcohols react on Cu(II) oxide at roughly 1.2–1.5 V.

What decides the products instead

The substrate

Copper oxides cut C–C bonds that sit next to OH or C=O groups. Glycerol, ethylene glycol and glucose give mostly formate, with glycolate or lactate on the side; ethanol stops at acetate. No copper anode joins carbons, so propionate or butyrate would have to come from 1-propanol, 1-butanol or their aldehydes, and the search found no copper study of those.

Potential, pH and phase

Below about 0.5 V vs RHE only metallic Cu works, and only on aldehydes (Liu et al., 2022). Above about 1.2 V the surface is Cu(II) oxide. pH decides whether glycerol stops at DHA or breaks to formate, and CuO beat Cu foil for formate at the same potential (Chuang & Lai, 2022).

Reconstruction and leaching

Cu2O facets oxidize to CuO under bias, and methanol can push CuO back to Cu2O. Polyols leach copper into solution, and one glucose route runs through dissolved Cu2+. By the time products are measured, the as-made facet is gone or partly dissolved.

How copper differs from your NiOOH

Copper is the better partner for the aldehyde-to-acid step and the weaker one for the alcohol-to-aldehyde step. Mixed NiOOH and Cu(OH)2 electrodes outperformed either oxide alone for HMF to FDCA (Woo et al., 2022).

Making the shapes

Starting recipes for a shape series. The Huang-group volumes come from later papers by the same group that say they follow the original procedure; check them against the original before relying on them.

Cu2O cubes, octahedra and rhombic dodecahedra

Kuo & Huang 2008; Huang et al. 2012; volumes as given in Chu & Huang 2017, ESI

Growth2 h, room temp.2 h, room temp.1 h, 32–34 °C
  1. Add water, CuCl2 and SDS; stir until clear.
  2. Add NaOH; a light-blue Cu(OH)2 suspension forms.
  3. Inject the NH2OH·HCl quickly (within about 3 s for rhombic dodecahedra), then leave undisturbed.
  4. Centrifuge; wash twice with 1:1 water:ethanol and once with ethanol. Avoid acid, which etches Cu2O.

Cu2O cubes to octahedra with PVP

Zhang et al. 2009; amounts from a 2011 RSC Adv. ESI using the same route

  1. 50 mL of 0.01 M CuCl2 at 55 °C with PVP (MW 30,000). PVP sets the shape: 4.44 g gives octahedra, 1.67 g truncated octahedra, less gives cube-like shapes.
  2. Add 5.0 mL of 2.0 M NaOH dropwise and stir 30 min.
  3. Add 5.0 mL of 0.6 M ascorbic acid dropwise and age 3 h at 55 °C.
  4. Wash repeatedly with water and ethanol; PVP is hard to remove, so check by FTIR before electrochemistry.

Cu(OH)2 and CuO nanowires on Cu foam

Wen, Zhang & Yang 2003; amounts from a patent citing this route

  1. Clean Cu foam in acetone, then dilute HCl; rinse.
  2. Immerse in 8.0 mL 10 M NaOH + 4.0 mL 1 M (NH4)2S2O8 + 18.0 mL water (2.67 M NaOH, 0.133 M persulfate) at room temperature for about 15 min. The surface turns light blue.
  3. Rinse and dry. Anneal in air at 200 °C for 1–2 h to convert the wires to CuO.

Electrodeposited Cu2O shapes

Siegfried & Choi 2004

  1. Cathodic deposition from Cu(NO3)2 at about 60 °C, pH 3.3–4.9, with SDS as the shape-directing additive.
  2. At 5 wt% SDS the pH sets the shape: cubes at pH 3.4, truncated cubes at 3.5, cuboctahedra at 3.7, truncated octahedra at 3.9.
  3. The Cu(NO3)2 concentration and deposition potential were not confirmed; take them from the paper.

For a glassy carbon electrode, use the same ink as in the Ni(OH)2 review: 5 mg catalyst with about 1 mg Vulcan carbon in 750 µL water, 250 µL isopropanol and 20 µL 5 wt% Nafion; 5 µL on a 3 mm disk gives about 0.35 mg cm⁻². Dry at or below 60 °C, since warm air oxidizes Cu2O.

If you need propionate or butyrate

Electro-oxidation on copper can only shorten or keep carbon chains, so C3–C6 VFAs from mixed organics come from acidogenic fermentation and microbial chain elongation (Agler et al., 2011; Angenent et al., 2016). In those processes electrochemistry is mostly used to extract and concentrate the acids, not to make them. A copper anode fits better downstream, turning a glycerol or ethanol side stream into formate or acetate.

How this was assembled

  • Compiled in October 2026 by six parallel literature searches, a second verification pass on every cited claim, and three searches dedicated to finding a counterexample to the short answer.
  • Publisher full texts were blocked in the research environment, so facts come from abstracts, landing pages and search excerpts of full texts and supporting information. Check numbers against the papers before citing them.
  • Potentials are vs RHE unless noted. Electrolytes and loadings differ, so values aren't comparable across rows.
  • Three items could not be confirmed: a 2025 Anal. Chim. Acta facet study, a 2021 ACS Catal. hollow-cuboctahedra sensor, and the authors of a Chem. Eng. J. study. Only the Chem. Eng. J. result is used here, and it is flagged.
